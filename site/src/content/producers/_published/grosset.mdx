Grosset was established in Clare Valley in the early 1980s, when its founder, identified in the record only by his first name, Jeffrey, began making wine there. The producer's own account of that period describes an approach built on questioning established method rather than following it.

<Pull attribution="Grosset, on their history page">
  Unconventional approach meant questioning everything from vineyard practices to smallest details of winemaking.
</Pull>

The vineyards sit in the high country of Clare Valley, at elevations the producer states run from 460 to 560 metres, among the coolest sites in the region. Grosset also draws fruit from Adelaide Hills, including from Lenswood and Piccadilly Valley, both named on wine labels in the record. Fruit source is estate throughout: the record states plainly that the fruit is hand-picked, organic and biodynamic.

Both organic and biodynamic status are given as practising, not certified. Neither the organic body (such as ACO, NASAA or AUS-QUAL) nor a biodynamic certifier (such as Demeter) is named on the page, so the claims stand as the producer's own statement rather than a verified certification. This is a common position among small growers, where certification carries an ongoing cost that does not always match the scale of the operation, though nothing in the record explains why Grosset in particular has not pursued it.

The record notes one effect the producer attributes to biodynamic management: an improvement in plant and insect diversity and complexity across the vineyards. No further detail on the method behind that claim is given, so it is reported here as a stated outcome rather than an established fact.

The current range spans riesling, fiano and chardonnay among the whites, and pinot noir, shiraz and cabernet among the reds, drawn across the two regions the producer works in. Named wines in the record include Polish Hill, Springvale and Alea rieslings from Clare Valley, an Apiana fiano, Lenswood and Piccadilly chardonnays from Adelaide Hills, an Adelaide Hills pinot noir, and Nereus and Gaia as Clare Valley red blends built around shiraz and cabernet respectively. None of the record's variety facts specify a wildferment, unfined, unfiltered or minimal-intervention approach; all four practice flags in the source data are recorded as false.

The record contains no address, no cellar door hours, no tasting fee and no information on visiting the property. It gives no pricing and no detail on how the wines are sold or distributed. On the strength of the founder narrative alone, with no parent company mentioned and no shared ownership signals found, the producer's independence is recorded as clear, though the page itself makes no explicit ownership statement to confirm it.
